Ch. 3 Knowledge systems and connectionism 1974–93

Knowledge systems and the return of connectionism (1974–1993)

Can expert knowledge be written as rules, and can multilayer networks be trained?

Key point

  • Two streams run side by side: the knowledge stream of frames (1974), scripts (1977), DENDRAL (1978) and the physical symbol system hypothesis (1976), and the connectionist stream of the Neocognitron (1980), the Hopfield network (1982), the Boltzmann machine (1985) and backpropagation (1986).
  • The backpropagation paper in Nature of 9 October 1986 has four references, one of them Perceptrons. Who wrote backpropagation first is treated in this chapter's box with both sides' bibliographies only.
  • Temporal-difference learning (1988), the Q-learning thesis (1989), the two universal approximation theorems (1988 and 1989), the forerunner of LeNet (1989) and the HMM tutorial (1989) crowd into two years. The "winter" at the chapter's end, in the early 1990s, is visible in the attendance series on the winters page.

Writing knowledge as structure

Minsky's "A Framework for Representing Knowledge", MIT AI Memo 306 of June 1974, proposed organizing knowledge in frames, remembered data structures for stereotyped situations: "A frame is a data-structure for representing a stereotyped situation, like being in a certain kind of living room, or going to a child's birthday party." Schank and Abelson's Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding (1977) developed the same idea as scripts, typical sequences of events; only chapter 1 was read in the 1977 pagination (a publisher's preview), and the card quotes the sentence on p. 19 that defines a script. Buchanan and Feigenbaum's "Dendral and Meta-Dendral" (August 1978, Artificial Intelligence) describes an expert system that infers molecular structure from mass-spectrometry data and a meta-system that learns its rules. These three are the original texts of the expert-system boom of the 1980s, whose size can be measured on the winters page.

Newell and Simon's "Computer science as empirical inquiry: symbols and search" was delivered as the Turing Award lecture on 20 October 1975 and printed in the March 1976 Communications of the ACM. "A physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action." That sentence has been read as the creed of symbolic AI; the references include McCarthy's LISP and Turing 1950.

Probability and statistics come in

The EM algorithm of Dempster, Laird and Rubin was read before the Royal Statistical Society on 8 December 1976 and printed in JRSS B in 1977. Its iteration of an expectation step and a maximization step for maximum-likelihood estimation from incomplete data is cited by Rabiner in this chapter and by Brown et al. (statistical machine translation) in chapter 4. Geman and Geman's "Stochastic Relaxation, Gibbs Distributions, and the Bayesian Restoration of Images" (November 1984) modeled images as Markov random fields and restored them with a Gibbs sampler combined with annealing; the annealing came from Kirkpatrick et al., "Optimization by Simulated Annealing" (13 May 1983, Science), and both are cited by the Boltzmann machine paper. Pearl's Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems (1988) wrote reasoning under uncertainty as belief networks; it is cited by Hinton et al. 2012 in chapter 5. It was read in a scan of the 1988 edition, and the card quotes the sentence on p. 13 saying that belief networks encode relevancies as neighboring nodes in a graph. Valiant's "A theory of the learnable" (1984; STOC preliminary version, then CACM in November) posed the question of which concept classes can be learned in a polynomial number of steps and became the starting point of learning theory; Cybenko 1989 cites it.

The return of connectionism

Fukushima's "Neocognitron" (April 1980, Biological Cybernetics) stacks alternating S-cell and C-cell layers and learns, without a teacher, recognition that is unaffected by shifts in position. LeCun et al. (1989) later in this chapter cite it, the oldest confirmed citation line in the convolutional lineage. Hopfield's "Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities" (April 1982, PNAS) is a model of asynchronously updated units whose collective dynamics flow to stable states and so act as a content-addressable memory; it cites McCulloch–Pitts, Hebb and Perceptrons, and is the work named in the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics citation. The Boltzmann machine of Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski (January 1985, Cognitive Science) added stochastic units and annealing to Hopfield's network and derived a rule that learns the constraints of a domain simply from examples.

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors" appeared in Nature on 9 October 1986: "We describe a new learning procedure, back-propagation, for networks of neurone-like units." Repeatedly adjusting weights to reduce the difference between actual and desired outputs makes hidden units come to represent features of the task. The paper has four references, one of them Perceptrons; it reads as an answer to the limits the 1969 book raised. Among the other papers in this version eight (Waibel 1989, Cortes 1995, Glorot 2010, Hinton 2012, both Mikolov 2013 papers, Sutskever 2014, LeCun 2015) list the Nature paper in their references; several others cite the PDP book chapter of the same year instead, and the cards' notes keep the two apart.

Werbos's "Applications of advances in nonlinear sensitivity analysis" (1982, IFIP proceedings) is known as the application of his 1974 thesis method to neural networks; it is behind a Springer paywall and was read in a copy of the printed volume obtained by the editor. The box on the priority of backpropagation follows below.

Reinforcement learning gets its name

Barto, Sutton and Anderson, "Neuronlike adaptive elements" (September 1983) learned to balance a pole on a cart with two elements, an associative search element and an adaptive critic element, from a failure signal alone; its references (Hebb, Widrow–Hoff, Minsky 1961, Samuel, McCulloch–Pitts) tie it most closely to chapters 1 and 2. Sutton's "Learning to predict by the methods of temporal differences" (August 1988, Machine Learning) defined temporal-difference methods, which assign credit by the difference between successive predictions, and proved convergence for special cases. The next year Watkins's Cambridge thesis "Learning from Delayed Rewards" (May 1989) presented a method that learns the optimal values of actions directly without a model of the environment, later called Q-learning. The 2024 Turing Award citation for Barto and Sutton points to Barto et al. 1983, Sutton 1988 and Sutton–Barto 1998 (chapter 4).

The height of symbolic AI, and its critique

Holland's Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems (1975, University of Michigan Press) is known as the original text of genetic algorithms; the 1975 edition was not seen, and the book was read in the 1992 MIT Press edition, which reprints the 1975 text; Sutton–Barto 1998 and Schmidhuber 2015 cite it. Searle's "Minds, brains, and programs" (September 1980, Behavioral and Brain Sciences) argued through the thought experiment of a person following rules for Chinese symbols without understanding them, the "Chinese room", that "instantiating a computer program is never by itself a sufficient condition of intentionality". Its references cite ELIZA and Schank and Abelson's scripts; it stands opposite the claim of "understanding" that Winograd represented in chapter 2. McDermott's R1 appeared first as a Carnegie Mellon technical report in April 1980 and in Artificial Intelligence in 1982: written in the production-system language OPS4, it configured DEC's VAX orders and was in regular use in a manufacturing organization; the report states that R1 uses match rather than generate-and-test, so little search is required. It is the record of an expert system in commercial use. Marr's Vision (1982) sets out three levels at which an information-processing device must be understood: computational theory, representation and algorithm, and hardware implementation. It was read in the 2010 MIT Press reissue, and the card quotes the sentence on p. 24 that sums up the three levels (whether the 1982 edition has the same page numbers was not verified).

Quinlan's "Induction of decision trees" (March 1986, the first issue of Machine Learning) described ID3, which chooses the attribute at each node by an information-based criterion. Symbolic learning got its journal in the same year as the return of neural networks; C4.5 in chapter 4 is its successor.

1989

Five papers appeared in journals in 1989 (two of them first as reports). Cybenko (December) and Hornik, Stinchcombe and White (a UCSD discussion paper in 1988, then Neural Networks in 1989) each proved that a feedforward network with one hidden layer can approximate any continuous function on the unit hypercube to any accuracy, given enough hidden units (the universal approximation theorems); Cybenko cites Hornik's 1988 preprint. LeCun et al., "Backpropagation Applied to Handwritten Zip Code Recognition" (December, Neural Computation) built the constraints of the task into the network and recognized handwritten U.S. Postal Service zip codes, the direct forerunner of LeNet (1998) in chapter 4. Rabiner's HMM tutorial (February, Proceedings of the IEEE) organized hidden Markov models for speech around three problems, and the time-delay neural network of Waibel et al. appeared first as an ATR technical report in October 1987 and in the journal in 1989; its sentence on 1946 test tokens — TDNN 98.5% against the best HMM's 93.7% — records neural networks beginning to compete with statistical models in speech. Elman's "Finding Structure in Time" (March 1990) closes the chapter with the simple recurrent network that feeds the hidden layer's pattern back as the next step's context.

What was and was not checked

Of 30 papers, 26 were read in the journal printing or an equivalent copy. Schank–Abelson was read only in chapter 1 (a publisher's preview), Pearl in a scan of the 1988 edition, Holland in the 1992 MIT Press edition and Marr in the 2010 MIT Press reissue; R1 was read in the technical-report version; Minsky 1974 was read in the author's web version, Elman in a retyped copy without page numbers, and LeCun 1985 in a scan of the proceedings paper with no printed page numbers. Werbos 1982 was read in a copy of the proceedings obtained by the editor. Candidates of the period that did not pass the rule include MYCIN (Shortliffe 1975), Kohonen's self-organizing map (1982), Brooks's subsumption architecture (1986) and Kowalski's "Algorithm = logic + control" (1979).

Multilayer feedforward networks are universal approximators

DOI · Neural Networks 2(5), 1989 (journal; Crossref published-print 1989-01, probably year-only); received 16 Sep 1988, revised and accepted 9 Mar 1989 (not public dates)

We show that multilayer feedforward networks with as few as one hidden layer are indeed capable of universal approximation in a very precise and satisfactory sense.p. 359

What changed. Proved, using the Stone-Weierstrass theorem and the cosine squasher of Gallant and White, that standard multilayer feedforward networks with as few as one hidden layer and arbitrary squashing functions can approximate any Borel measurable function between finite-dimensional spaces to any desired accuracy, given sufficiently many hidden units.
