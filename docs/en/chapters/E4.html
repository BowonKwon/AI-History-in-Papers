Ch. 4 Statistical learning 1990–2011

Statistical learning (1990–2011)

Can generalization from data be guaranteed?

Key point

  • The originals of the 1990s ask about guarantees. Support-vector networks (1995), the lasso (1996), random forests (2001) and conditional random fields (2001) tried to answer with statistics why learning generalizes, while Q-learning (1992), REINFORCE (1992) and policy gradients (1999) laid down the mathematics of reinforcement learning.
  • Neural networks did not vanish from this chapter. Hochreiter's 1991 thesis analyzed vanishing gradients; LSTM and bidirectional RNNs (1997) and LeNet (1998) followed; the two deep-belief-network papers of 2006 and ImageNet (2009) set the conditions for chapter 5.
  • Statistical machine translation (1990) and the neural language model (2003) head the longest lineage on the site, the one that runs to the language models of chapter 7. In the 1990s that convention calls a winter, papers with "neural network" in the title were increasing (winters page).

Language as probability

Brown et al., "A statistical approach to machine translation" (June 1990, Computational Linguistics) set translation as choosing the source sentence S that maximizes Pr(S)Pr(T|S), a language model times a translation model. That one-line decomposition is the skeleton of thirty years of statistical and neural translation; the paper cites the EM algorithm of chapter 3. At ICML in June 2001, conditional random fields (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira) modeled the labeling and segmentation of sequences as conditional probabilities, avoiding the label bias of maximum-entropy Markov models; in 2003, in JMLR, latent Dirichlet allocation (Blei, Ng and Jordan) set up a three-level Bayesian model with each document a mixture of topics. The same journal, the same year, carried Bengio et al., "A Neural Probabilistic Language Model" (February 2003), which learned a distributed representation for each word and the probability of word sequences in one network. Its sentence on fighting the curse of dimensionality by learning a distributed representation for words is where word2vec in chapter 5 and the language models of chapter 6 point back.

The statistics of generalization

Boser, Guyon and Vapnik (July 1992, COLT) is the training algorithm for optimal-margin classifiers; the original could not be opened for this version. Its extension to non-separable data, Cortes and Vapnik, "Support-vector networks" (September 1995, Machine Learning), was read: "The support-vector network is a new learning machine for two-group classification problems." Inputs are mapped non-linearly into a very high-dimensional feature space where a linear decision surface is drawn; the reference list includes the 1986 Nature backpropagation paper (under a misprinted title). Tibshirani's lasso (1996, JRSS B) estimates linear models under a bound on the sum of absolute coefficients, so that some coefficients become exactly zero; Breiman's "Random Forests" (October 2001) classifies by the vote of trees grown with independent, identically distributed random vectors and shows that the generalization error converges as trees are added. All three ask in mathematics why learning generalizes: the 1990s' answers to Valiant's question in chapter 2.

The mathematics of reinforcement learning

Two papers appeared in one issue of Machine Learning in May 1992. Watkins and Dayan, "Q-learning" completed the convergence proof the 1989 thesis had only outlined: if every action is sampled repeatedly in every state, the action values converge to the optimum with probability 1. Williams's REINFORCE showed that the weight adjustments of a network of stochastic units follow the gradient of expected reinforcement, and cites Barto 1983 from chapter 3. Tesauro's TD-Gammon (March 1995, CACM) reported a network that trained itself by self-play with TD(λ) to play backgammon; both DQN papers in chapter 5 cite it. The ACM printing could not be opened, so it was read in an authorized transcription. Kaelbling, Littman and Cassandra (May 1998, Artificial Intelligence) brought partially observable Markov decision processes to the problem of choosing actions in AI. Sutton and Barto's Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (1998, MIT Press) gathered the lineage in one framework, as its preface promises "a clear and simple account of the key ideas and algorithms", and is the most-cited book in this version, cited by sixteen other papers. The next year the policy-gradient theorem of Sutton et al. (NIPS 12) represented the policy by its own function approximator and showed that the gradient of expected reward can be estimated with an approximate action-value function. AlphaGo in chapter 5 cites both.

Neural networks continued

Hochreiter's diploma thesis at TU Munich (15 June 1991, in German) analyzed why backpropagation in recurrent networks fails to learn from inputs far in the past, by following the backward flow of error signals, and proposed memory units. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (March 1994, IEEE TNN) showed the same problem by another route: the longer the time span of the dependencies, the harder gradient learning becomes. The answer to both analyses is Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, "Long Short-Term Memory" (1 November 1997, Neural Computation): constant error flow inside units whose access is controlled by multiplicative gates, bridging lags of more than 1000 steps. The bidirectional RNN of Schuster and Paliwal appeared the same month. Jacobs et al., "Adaptive Mixtures of Local Experts" (February 1991) combined several expert networks with a gating network, the original text to which the sparse mixtures of experts in chapter 6 point back.

LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner, "Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition" (November 1998, Proceedings of the IEEE) wrote that hand-crafted feature extraction can be replaced by learning machines operating directly on pixel images. This paper (LeNet), continuing the 1989 zip-code paper of chapter 3, describes a deployed bank-check reader. Hinton's contrastive divergence (technical report 2000; Neural Computation, August 2002) gave a way to train products of experts, and three papers followed in 2006. The deep belief network of Hinton, Osindero and Teh (July 2006) learned deep networks one layer at a time using complementary priors; Hinton and Salakhutdinov (28 July 2006, Science) showed with the same layer-wise pretraining that deep autoencoders learn low-dimensional codes better than PCA; Bengio et al. (NIPS 2006, December) confirmed experimentally the hypothesis that the greedy layer-wise strategy "mostly helps the optimization". Glorot and Bengio's initialization (May 2010, AISTATS) and Nair and Hinton's rectified linear units (June 2010, ICML) supplied two handles that let deep networks train without pretraining. Cireşan et al. (arXiv v1 1 March 2010; Neural Computation) reported 0.35% error on MNIST from large, plain multilayer perceptrons trained on GPUs without unsupervised pretraining.

Search, games, data

Campbell, Hoane and Hsu, "Deep Blue" (January 2002) described the search engine, evaluation function and game database of the machine that beat Kasparov in 1997. In 2006 another road opened in Go: Coulom (May, Turin) combined tree search with Monte Carlo evaluation through a backup operator that moves from averaging to minimax, and the UCT of Kocsis and Szepesvári (September, ECML) guided that search with bandit ideas. AlphaGo's search in chapter 5 comes from these two. The recurrent-network language model of Mikolov et al. (26 September 2010, Interspeech) and Collobert et al., "Natural Language Processing (almost) from Scratch" (arXiv 2 March 2011; JMLR) are the two points where feature engineering in language processing began to give way to learned representations. And Deng et al., "ImageNet" (June 2009, CVPR) reported 3.2 million images organized by the WordNet hierarchy and collected through Mechanical Turk. Chapter 5 does not begin without this database.

What was and was not checked

Of 34 papers, 30 were read in the original. Boser 1992 was not read; Bengio 1994 was read in an author preprint, Russell–Norvig in the 4th-edition preface, Deep Blue in the authors' manuscript of August 2001, and Sutton–Barto 1998 in the authors' HTML of the first edition, which has no page numbers. Candidates of the period that did not pass the rule include C4.5 (1993), bagging (1996), AdaBoost (1997), PageRank (1999), SIFT (1999), gradient boosting (2001), Viola–Jones (2001), Breiman's "two cultures" (2001), Stanley (2006), "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data" (2009) and Watson (2010). Several classics of statistical learning are missing, and we record it as a limit of a reference set weighted toward neural-network textbooks.

What changed.
