Ch. 4 Statistical learning 1990–2011

Statistical learning (1990–2011)

Can generalization from data be guaranteed?

Key point

  • The originals of the 1990s ask about guarantees. Support-vector networks (1995), the lasso (1996), random forests (2001) and conditional random fields (2001) tried to answer with statistics why learning generalizes, while Q-learning (1992), REINFORCE (1992) and policy gradients (1999) laid down the mathematics of reinforcement learning.
  • Neural networks did not vanish from this chapter. Hochreiter's 1991 thesis analyzed vanishing gradients; LSTM and bidirectional RNNs (1997) and LeNet (1998) followed; the two deep-belief-network papers of 2006 and ImageNet (2009) set the conditions for chapter 5.
  • Statistical machine translation (1990) and the neural language model (2003) head the longest lineage on the site, the one that runs to the language models of chapter 7. In the 1990s that convention calls a winter, papers with "neural network" in the title were increasing (winters page).

Language as probability

Brown et al., "A statistical approach to machine translation" (June 1990, Computational Linguistics) set translation as choosing the source sentence S that maximizes Pr(S)Pr(T|S), a language model times a translation model. That one-line decomposition is the skeleton of thirty years of statistical and neural translation; the paper cites the EM algorithm of chapter 3. At ICML in June 2001, conditional random fields (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira) modeled the labeling and segmentation of sequences as conditional probabilities, avoiding the label bias of maximum-entropy Markov models; in 2003, in JMLR, latent Dirichlet allocation (Blei, Ng and Jordan) set up a three-level Bayesian model with each document a mixture of topics. The same journal, the same year, carried Bengio et al., "A Neural Probabilistic Language Model" (February 2003), which learned a distributed representation for each word and the probability of word sequences in one network. Its sentence on fighting the curse of dimensionality by learning a distributed representation for words is where word2vec in chapter 5 and the language models of chapter 6 point back.

The statistics of generalization

Boser, Guyon and Vapnik (July 1992, COLT) is the training algorithm for optimal-margin classifiers; it was read in the authors' typeset copy, which has no printed page numbers. Its extension to non-separable data, Cortes and Vapnik, "Support-vector networks" (September 1995, Machine Learning), was read: "The support-vector network is a new learning machine for two-group classification problems." Inputs are mapped non-linearly into a very high-dimensional feature space where a linear decision surface is drawn; the reference list includes the 1986 Nature backpropagation paper (under a misprinted title). Tibshirani's lasso (1996, JRSS B) estimates linear models under a bound on the sum of absolute coefficients, so that some coefficients become exactly zero; Breiman's "Random Forests" (October 2001) classifies by the vote of trees grown with independent, identically distributed random vectors and shows that the generalization error converges as trees are added. All three ask in mathematics why learning generalizes: answers of the 1990s and 2001 to Valiant's question in chapter 3.

The mathematics of reinforcement learning

Two papers appeared in one issue of Machine Learning in May 1992. Watkins and Dayan, "Q-learning" completed the convergence proof the 1989 thesis had only outlined: with action values represented discretely and every action sampled repeatedly in every state (under conditions on the learning rate), the action values converge to the optimum with probability 1. Williams's REINFORCE showed that the weight adjustments of a network of stochastic units follow the gradient of expected reinforcement, and cites Barto 1983 from chapter 3. Tesauro's TD-Gammon (March 1995, CACM) reported a network that trained itself by self-play with TD(λ) to play backgammon; both DQN papers in chapter 5 cite it. The ACM printing could not be opened, so it was read in an authorized transcription. Kaelbling, Littman and Cassandra (May 1998, Artificial Intelligence) brought partially observable Markov decision processes to the problem of choosing actions in AI. Sutton and Barto's Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (1998, MIT Press) gathered the lineage in one framework, as its preface promises "a clear and simple account of the key ideas and algorithms", and is the most-cited book in this version, cited by twelve other papers. The next year the policy-gradient theorem of Sutton et al. (NIPS 12) represented the policy by its own function approximator and showed that the gradient of expected reward can be estimated with an approximate action-value function. AlphaGo in chapter 5 cites both.

Neural networks continued

Hochreiter's diploma thesis at TU Munich (15 June 1991, in German) analyzed why backpropagation in recurrent networks fails to learn from inputs far in the past, by following the backward flow of error signals, and proposed memory units. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (March 1994, IEEE TNN) showed the same problem by another route: the longer the time span of the dependencies, the harder gradient learning becomes. The answer to both analyses is Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, "Long Short-Term Memory" (1 November 1997, Neural Computation): constant error flow inside units whose access is controlled by multiplicative gates, bridging lags of more than 1000 steps. The bidirectional RNN of Schuster and Paliwal appeared the same month. Jacobs et al., "Adaptive Mixtures of Local Experts" (February 1991) combined several expert networks with a gating network, the original text to which the sparse mixtures of experts in chapter 6 point back.

LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner, "Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition" (November 1998, Proceedings of the IEEE) wrote that hand-crafted feature extraction can be replaced by learning machines operating directly on pixel images. This paper (LeNet), continuing the 1989 zip-code paper of chapter 3, describes a deployed bank-check reader. Hinton's contrastive divergence (technical report 2000; Neural Computation, August 2002) gave a way to train products of experts, and three papers followed in 2006. The deep belief network of Hinton, Osindero and Teh (July 2006) learned deep networks one layer at a time using complementary priors; Hinton and Salakhutdinov (28 July 2006, Science) showed with the same layer-wise pretraining that deep autoencoders learn low-dimensional codes better than PCA; Bengio et al. (NIPS 2006, December) confirmed experimentally the hypothesis that the greedy layer-wise strategy "mostly helps the optimization". Glorot and Bengio's initialization (May 2010, AISTATS) and Nair and Hinton's rectified linear units (June 2010, ICML) supplied two handles later deep networks use: an initialization scheme and the rectified unit (introduced here in RBMs). Raina, Madhavan and Ng (June 2009, ICML) argued that graphics processors far surpass multicore CPUs and could change the applicability of deep unsupervised learning, reporting deep-belief-network training up to 70 times faster; its references cite both Hinton papers of 2006. Cireşan et al. (arXiv v1 1 March 2010; Neural Computation) reported 0.35% error on MNIST from large, plain multilayer perceptrons trained on GPUs without unsupervised pretraining.

Search, games, data

Campbell, Hoane and Hsu, "Deep Blue" (January 2002) described the search engine, evaluation function and game database of the machine that beat Kasparov in 1997. In 2006 another road opened in Go: Coulom (May, Turin) combined tree search with Monte Carlo evaluation through a backup operator that moves from averaging to minimax, and the UCT of Kocsis and Szepesvári (September, ECML) guided that search with bandit ideas. AlphaGo's search in chapter 5 comes from these two. The recurrent-network language model of Mikolov et al. (26 September 2010, Interspeech) and Collobert et al., "Natural Language Processing (almost) from Scratch" (arXiv 2 March 2011; JMLR) are the two points where feature engineering in language processing began to give way to learned representations. And Deng et al., "ImageNet" (June 2009, CVPR) reported 3.2 million images organized by the WordNet hierarchy and collected through Mechanical Turk. Chapter 5 does not begin without this database.

Boosting, and two events

Schapire's "The strength of weak learnability" (FOCS 1989; Machine Learning, 1990) proved constructively that a weak learner, only slightly better than random guessing, is equivalent to a strong learner of arbitrarily high accuracy; it cites Valiant 1984 from chapter 3. AdaBoost (Freund and Schapire) (EuroCOLT, March 1995; JCSS, 1997) derived from multiplicative weight updates a boosting algorithm that needs no prior knowledge of the weak learner's performance; this version read the authors' manuscript. Conditional random fields in this chapter and the candidate Viola–Jones cite it. Quinlan's C4.5 (1993), the book that followed ID3 of chapter 3, was read only in a 16-page publisher's preview (the start of chapter 1). Breiman's "Bagging predictors" appeared first as Berkeley Statistics technical report 421 in September 1994 and in Machine Learning in August 1996: predictors built on bootstrap replicates and combined by averaging or plurality vote, with the finding that accuracy improves when the prediction method is unstable, the forerunner of random forests (2001).

Two events stand at the end of the statistical-learning era. "Stanley" (Thrun et al.) (25 September 2006, Journal of Field Robotics) describes the autonomous vehicle that first completed the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge desert course, with software the paper says was built on machine learning and probabilistic reasoning. "Checkers Is Solved" (Schaeffer et al.) (19 July 2007, Science) announced a computational proof, from endgame databases and proof-tree search run almost continuously since 1989, that checkers is weakly solved: perfect play by both sides from the starting position is a draw. Its references include Shannon 1950: one end of the lineage of measuring intelligence by games that began in chapter 1.

What was and was not checked

Of 51 papers, 47 were read in the original. C4.5 was read only in a 16-page publisher's preview (the start of chapter 1); Raina 2009 was read in an archived copy of the ICML proceedings, bagging in the journal's open-access PDF, and Boser 1992 in the authors' manuscript. Bengio 1994 was read in an author preprint, Russell–Norvig in the 4th-edition preface, and Sutton–Barto 1998 in the authors' HTML of the first edition, which has no page numbers. Gradient boosting (Friedman) and trajectory averaging in stochastic approximation (Polyak and Juditsky 1992), both added in 1.1, were read in copies of the journal printing obtained by the editor; latent semantic indexing (Deerwester et al.) was read in the authors' manuscript. Adding Murphy's textbook to the reference set in 1.1 brought gradient boosting and Watson (2010) over the textbook rule, and SIFT (1999) entered by the lineage rule. PageRank (1999), Viola–Jones (2001), Breiman's "two cultures" (2001) and "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data" (2009) still did not pass. Retrieval papers are still missing, and we record it as a limit of a reference set made of AI and machine-learning textbooks.

We use near state-of-the-art supervised baselines, and find that each of the three word representations improves the accuracy of these baselines.p. 384

The system we have built and are continuing to develop, called DeepQA, is a massively parallel probabilistic evidence-based architecture.p. 68

What changed.
