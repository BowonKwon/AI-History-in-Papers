Ch. 2 Symbols and perceptrons 1956–74

Symbolic AI and the first neural networks (1956–1974)

Can intelligence be written as search and logic, and how much can a perceptron learn?

Key point

  • The perceptron of 1958 and LISP of 1960 share a chapter. A learning machine written in probability and symbol processing written in recursive functions: the two later traditions each got their original text within two years.
  • Samuel's checkers program (1959) left the sentence that it learned to play better than its author, and Widrow and Hoff's adaptive element (1960) described training by examples. Both are cited by the reinforcement-learning papers of chapter 3.
  • Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons (1969) and Werbos's thesis (1974) could not be obtained for this version, which is why their cards carry no quotation. Whether the chapter ends in a "first winter" is examined with numbers on the winters page.

Bellman's Dynamic Programming (Princeton, 1957) opens the chapter. The book was read in a scan of the sixth printing (1972) of the 1957 edition, and the card quotes the principle of optimality on p. 83. Sutton (1988) in chapter 3 and the Sutton–Barto textbook (1998) in chapter 4 cite it in their reference lists. It is placed as the root of the reinforcement-learning lineage.

Rosenblatt's "The perceptron" appeared in Psychological Review in November 1958. What McCulloch and Pitts had written in symbolic logic, Rosenblatt rewrote in probability theory: for "a hypothetical nervous system, or machine, called a perceptron", a system of randomly connected units can learn to associate specific responses with specific stimuli. Its references list both Hebb 1949 and McCulloch–Pitts 1943; the two traditions that ran separately in chapter 1 meet here.

The next year Samuel's "Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers" (July 1959, IBM Journal) applied two procedures to checkers, rote learning and a generalization procedure that adjusts the coefficients of a scoring polynomial. The sentence reads: enough work has been done to verify that a computer can be programmed to learn to play a better game of checkers than the person who wrote the program. Samuel cited Shannon's chess paper and Rosenblatt; Sutton (1988) and Watkins (1989) in chapter 3 cite Samuel. These four papers are the oldest solid lines of the reinforcement-learning lineage.

Two points fall in 1960. In the April Communications of the ACM, McCarthy's "Recursive functions of symbolic expressions" described LISP, a programming system for the IBM 704, through recursive functions of symbolic expressions and conditional expressions; it was read in a scan of the ACM printing, and the quotation is on p. 184. Widrow and Hoff's "Adaptive switching circuits", a technical report of 30 June, proposed an adaptive neuron element whose gains are adjusted to minimize average error, trained by "showing it examples of inputs and desired outputs". Sutton 1988 and Barto 1983 in chapter 3 cite the report.

Minsky's "Steps toward Artificial Intelligence" (1961, Proceedings of the IRE) surveyed search, pattern recognition, learning, planning and induction in one article and is cited by Barto 1983. It appeared in the January 1961 issue.

Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons (1969, MIT Press) is the most-cited book in this chapter: Rumelhart 1986, Hopfield 1982, Ackley 1985, Cybenko 1989 and Minsky 1974 all cite it. The book defines the order of a predicate and proves that parity has order |R| and that connectedness is not of finite order. The 1969 printing was not obtained, so the book was read in the 1988 expanded edition, whose copyright page says it keeps the 1969 text with handwritten alterations made for the 1972 second printing; whether its page numbers match the first printing was not verified. The card quotes the sentence on p. 232 that leaves as a research problem the authors' judgment that extending the perceptron to many layers is sterile.

Language and backpropagation close the chapter. Winograd's "Understanding natural language" first appeared as MIT AI Technical Report 235 in January 1971 and in Cognitive Psychology in 1972: a system (SHRDLU) that handles English dialogue about a simple robot world with knowledge represented as procedures. Werbos's Harvard thesis "Beyond Regression" (1974) is known for applying chain-rule adjustment of multilayer-network weights to prediction problems in the behavioral sciences; no public copy exists, the card is empty, and the full text is in a 1994 Wiley volume. The priority of backpropagation is treated in a box in chapter 3.

The originals of symbolic AI

McCarthy's "Programs with Common Sense" was read at the National Physical Laboratory symposium at Teddington in November 1958 and printed in the 1959 proceedings. It proposed the "advice taker", a program that keeps its knowledge and heuristics as sentences of the predicate calculus and draws immediate conclusions from them, and defined a program as having common sense "if it automatically deduces for itself a sufficiently wide class of immediate consequences" of what it is told and already knows. The 1960 LISP paper and McCarthy–Hayes 1969 cite it. The same winter, Newell, Shaw and Simon's "Report on a general problem-solving program" (RAND P-1584, written 30 December 1958, revised 9 February 1959) described GPS, which separates the content of a problem from general solving techniques and applies means-ends analysis and planning through recursive goals; its references include the 1956 Logic Theorist. Robinson's resolution principle (January 1965, JACM) gave first-order logic a complete theorem-proving system with a single inference rule that merges substitution and truth-functional analysis; Winograd 1972 cites it. Zadeh's "Fuzzy sets" (June 1965, Information and Control) defined sets whose grades of membership run continuously between 0 and 1; this version read only the abstract, so the card is "partial". Weizenbaum's ELIZA (January 1966, CACM) imitated English conversation with decomposition and reassembly rules triggered by keywords, and separated the rules into editable "scripts"; Searle in chapter 3 and Generative Agents in chapter 7 cite it.

A* (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael) (July 1968, IEEE Trans. SSC) set up a formal framework in which an estimate of the remaining path cost guides graph search, and proved that when the estimate never exceeds the true cost (is admissible), A* expands no more nodes than any admissible algorithm using the same information (ties apart). Its references include Samuel 1959; Pearl 1988 in chapter 3 and Tree of Thoughts in chapter 7 cite it. McCarthy and Hayes, "Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence" (1969, Machine Intelligence 4) formalized situations, fluents and actions in first-order logic and named the "frame problem"; its references cite McCarthy 1959, Minsky 1961, Shannon 1950 and Turing 1950, the chapter's most connected paper to chapter 1. STRIPS (Fikes and Nilsson) appeared first as SRI Technical Note 43 in October 1970 and in Artificial Intelligence in 1971: a problem solver that represents world models as sets of predicate-calculus formulas and finds operator sequences by means-ends analysis. It cites McCarthy–Hayes, and SayCan and Inner Monologue in chapter 7 cite it as the original text of planning.

The chapter ends with the Lighthill report. "Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey", written for the UK Science Research Council in July 1972 and published in 1973 with replies, divided AI research into advanced automation (A), central-nervous-system studies (C) and the bridge of building robots (B), and wrote: "In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised." It named the combinatorial explosion as the main cause and mentions Winograd 1972 in the text. That the report cut British AI support is widely accepted; as the winters page says, no yearly amounts are in the sources.

What was and was not checked

Of the 20 papers, the original printings of Rosenblatt 1958, Samuel, Widrow–Hoff (WESCON convention record), Robinson, Weizenbaum and A* were read, GPS in the RAND report scan, McCarthy 1959 and 1969 in the author's re-typeset copies, Zadeh in the abstract only, STRIPS in the technical note and the journal pages, Winograd in the 1972 journal version, and Werbos 1974 in a copy of the thesis obtained by the editor. Rosenblatt's Principles of Neurodynamics, added in 1.1, was read in the 1961 Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory report of the same text (VG-1196-G-8), and Hubel–Wiesel 1962 in the page scans on PubMed Central. Bellman was read in a scan of the sixth printing of the 1957 edition and Minsky–Papert in the 1988 expanded edition. Among the period's candidates, Newell, Shaw and Simon's Logic Theorist (1956), the Dartmouth proposal (1955), Selfridge's Pandemonium (1959), the multilayer learning of Ivakhnenko (1965) and Amari (1967), and Quillian's semantic memory (1968) did not pass the two-textbook rule.

Dynamic Programming

Richard Bellman · First public 1957 (book) · Venue: Princeton University Press (A RAND Corporation research study) · Pages xxv + 340 pp. · Citations (OpenAlex) 14,589 (checked 2026-11-03)

URL · Dover reprint 2003; manifest DOI 10.1126/science.153.3731.34 is a different work: Bellman, 'Dynamic Programming', Science 153(3731):34–37, July 1966 (Crossref)

An optimal policy has the property that whatever the initial state and initial decision are, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision.p. 83

What changed. Introduces the term "dynamic programming" for the mathematical theory of multi-stage decision processes and recasts such processes so that, instead of the optimal sequence of decisions from one fixed state, one seeks the optimal decision at any state, reducing the dimension of the problem to that of the decision at a single stage. Derives functional equations from a principle of optimality, applies them to deterministic and stochastic multi-stage processes such as allocation problems, and introduces approximation in policy space.

Cited by (within this list): Minsky 1961, Moore & Atkeson 1993, Schmidhuber 2014, Sutton 1988, Sutton & Barto 1998

Programs with Common Sense

What changed. The perceptron: A probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain

URL · Revised RAND P-1584 9 Feb 1959; Proceedings of the International Conference on Information Processing (UNESCO, Paris, June 1959), pp. 256–264 (per OpenAlex/dblp record, not seen)

GPS, the program described in this paper, contributes to the theory of problem solving by embodying two very general systems of heuristics — means-ends analysis and planning — within an organization that allows them to be applied to varying subject matters.p. 2

Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers

Arthur L. Samuel · First public 1959-07 (journal issue) · Venue: IBM Journal of Research and Development, 3(3) · Pages 210–229 · Citations (OpenAlex) 4,444 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Reprinted IBM J. Res. Dev. 44(1/2), 2000 (not checked); book reprint pp. 535ff marked 'Originally published in IBM Journal, Vol. 3, No. 3, July, 1959'

Enough work has been done to verify the fact that a computer can be programmed so that it will learn to play a better game of checkers than can be played by the person who wrote the program.p. 211

What changed. Investigates two machine-learning procedures, rote learning and a generalization procedure that adjusts the terms of a scoring polynomial, using checkers. Adaptive switching circuits

Bernard Carlos Widrow, Marcian E. Hoff · First public 1960-06-30 (tech report) · Venue: IRE WESCON Convention Record, 1960, Part 4 · Pages 96–104 · Citations (OpenAlex) 4,009 (checked 2026-11-03)

An approach to such problems is taken in this paper which does not require an explicit use of the truth table.p. 96

What changed. Proposes adaptive switching circuits designed to minimize the average number of errors, using an adaptive neuron element whose gains are set during a training procedure by performance feedback. The designer trains the system by showing it examples of inputs and desired outputs.

Steps toward Artificial Intelligence

Marvin Minsky · First public 1961-01 (journal issue) · Venue: Proceedings of the IRE, 49(1) · Pages 8–30 · Citations (OpenAlex) 1,704 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Received by the IRE 24 Oct 1960 (footnote, not a public date)

Summary—The problems of heuristic programming—of making computers solve really difficult problems—are divided into five main areas: Search, Pattern-Recognition, Learning, Planning, and Induction.p. 8

What changed. Organized the problems of heuristic programming into five areas (Search, Pattern-Recognition, Learning, Planning, and Induction) and reviewed the problem-solving programs and literature of the time under that scheme. Principles of Neurodynamics: Perceptrons and the Theory of Brain Mechanisms

Frank Rosenblatt · First public 1961-03-15 (tech report) · Venue: Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, Inc., Buffalo, N.Y., Report No. VG-1196-G-8 (book edition: Spartan Books, 1962) · Pages front matter (preface pp. vii–ix, contents from p. xi) + pp. 1–616 + distribution list (626 PDF pages) · Citations (OpenAlex) 2,210 (checked 2026-11-03)

URL · Published as a book, Principles of Neurodynamics, Spartan Books, Washington, D.C., 1962 (per manifest; book not read); reviewed by A. A. Mullin, American Mathematical Monthly 70(5): 586, May 1963 (DOI 10.2307/2312103), the OpenAlex record that carries this work's citations

Receptive fields, binocular interaction and functional architecture in the cat's visual cortex

David Hunter Hubel, T. N. Wiesel · First public 1962-01 (journal issue) · Venue: The Journal of Physiology, 160(1) · Pages 106–154 · Citations (OpenAlex) 13,602 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Received 31 July 1961 (not a public date)

It is suggested that columns containing cells with common receptive-field axis orientations are functional units, in which cells with simple fields represent an early stage in organization, possibly receiving their afferents directly from lateral geniculate cells, and cells with complex fields are of higher order, receiving projections from a number of cells with simple fields within the same column.p. 152 (Summary, point 8)

What changed. Recorded single cells in the visual cortex of anesthetized cats and sorted their receptive fields into "simple" and "complex" types, with stimulus orientation critical for both. Presented in this paper is a formulation of first-order logic which is specifically designed for use as the basic theoretical instrument of a computer theorem-proving program.p. 23

Fuzzy sets

L. A. Zadeh · First public 1965-06 (journal issue) · Venue: Information and Control 8(3), June 1965 · Pages 338–353 (per Crossref; not seen) · Citations (OpenAlex) 67,876 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Elsevier open-archive license from 2014-05-15 (Crossref license record)

A fuzzy set is a class of objects with a continuum of grades of membership.page unknown (abstract as shown on the publisher page)

What changed. Defined a fuzzy set as a class of objects with a continuum of grades of membership, characterized by a membership function with values between zero and one. ELIZA—a computer program for the study of natural language communication between man and machine

Joseph Weizenbaum · First public 1966-01 (journal issue) · Venue: Communications of the ACM 9(1), January 1966 · Pages 36–45 · Citations (OpenAlex) 4,452 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Received Sept 1965

ELIZA is a program which makes natural language conversation with a computer possible.p. 36

What changed. Described ELIZA, a program on MIT's MAC time-sharing system in which input sentences are analyzed by decomposition rules triggered by key words and responses are generated by reassembly rules, with the rules supplied as editable 'scripts'. Discussed identification of key words, minimal context, transformations, responses without key words, and psychological issues of the approach.

A Formal Basis for the Heuristic Determination of Minimum Cost Paths

Peter Hart, Nils J. Nilsson, Bertram Raphael · First public 1968-07 (journal issue) · Venue: IEEE Transactions on Systems Science and Cybernetics SSC-4(2), July 1968 · Pages 100–107 · Citations (OpenAlex) 12,743 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Manuscript received 1967-11-24

This paper describes how heuristic information from the problem domain can be incorporated into a formal mathematical theory of graph searching and demonstrates an optimality property of a class of search strategies.p. 100

What changed. Gave a formal framework in which an estimate of remaining path cost drawn from the problem domain guides graph search, defining the algorithm A* and conditions under which it is admissible. Showed that A* expands no more nodes than any admissible algorithm using no more information from the problem domain (apart from ties).

Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence

John McCarthy, Patrick J. Hayes · First public 1969 (book) · Venue: Machine Intelligence 4, ed. B. Meltzer and D. Michie, Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1969 · Pages 51 pp. (author's web PDF); printed pages 463–502 per author bibliography (not seen) · Citations (OpenAlex) 3,280 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI

A computer program capable of acting intelligently in the world must have a general representation of the world in terms of which its inputs are interpreted.p. 1 (author's web PDF)

What changed. Argued that building an intelligent program requires an epistemologically adequate representation of the world, and explained 'can', 'causes' and 'knows' using a model of interacting automata. Formalised situations, fluents, actions, strategies and knowledge in first-order logic so that it can be proved that a strategy achieves a goal, and discussed the difficulties of this formalism, including the frame problem.

Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry

Marvin Minsky, Seymour A. Papert · First public 1969 (book) · Venue: MIT Press (Cambridge, Mass.) · Pages vii–xv, 1–287 + index (expanded edition: 1988 prologue pp. vii–xv; chapters 0–13; 1988 epilogue; bibliographic notes pp. 281–287) · Citations (OpenAlex) 3,248 (checked 2026-11-03)

· Expanded edition, MIT Press 1988; MIT Press ebook listed on IEEE Xplore (document 8093962)

Nevertheless, we consider it to be an important research problem to elucidate (or reject) our intuitive judgment that the extension is sterile.p. 232 (1988 expanded edition)

What changed. Studies perceptrons, machines that compute a predicate as a linear threshold function of simpler partial predicates, as the simplest machines with a clear claim to be parallel, with the aim of understanding concepts of the general theory of computation; it defines the order of a predicate and, using group-invariance arguments, proves that parity has order |R| and that connectedness is not of finite order. Also treats coefficient size and learning, and states the authors' judgment that extending the perceptron to many layers is sterile, posing its confirmation or rejection as a research problem.

STRIPS: A new approach to the application of theorem proving to problem solving

Richard E. Fikes, Nils J. Nilsson · First public 1970-10 (tech report) · Venue: Artificial Intelligence 2(3–4), 1971 (North-Holland) · Pages 189–208 · Citations (OpenAlex) 4,880 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · 2nd IJCAI, London, 1971-09-01/03; Artificial Intelligence 2(3–4) 1971 (Crossref 1971-12)

This paper describes a new problem-solving program called STRIPS (STanford Research Institute Problem Solver).p. 189

What changed. Described STRIPS, a problem solver that searches a space of world models, each a collection of first-order predicate calculus formulas, for a sequence of operators reaching a model in which the goal formula can be proven. It uses a resolution theorem prover to answer questions about particular models and means-ends analysis to guide the search.

Understanding natural language

Terry A. Winograd · First public 1971-01 (tech report) · Venue: Cognitive Psychology, 3(1) · Pages 1–191 · Citations (OpenAlex) 2,807 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Dissertation submitted 24 Aug 1970 (not a public date); Cognitive Psychology 3(1):1–191, 1972 (Crossref 10.1016/0010-0285(72)90002-3 published-print 1972-01); the journal's own reference list cites the report as 'MAC-TR-84, Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, MIT, February 1971'; Academic Press book 1972

This paper describes a computer system for understanding English.p. 1

What changed. Describes a computer system that answers questions, executes commands, and accepts information in an interactive English dialog with a simulated robot that manipulates toy blocks, combining a parser, a recognition grammar of English, programs for semantic analysis and a general problem solving system. Knowledge is represented as procedures, with special procedural representations for syntax, semantics, and inference.

Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey

James Lighthill · First public 1973 (tech report) · Venue: Artificial Intelligence: a paper symposium, Part I, London: Science Research Council, 1973 · Pages page unknown (web transcription without printed page numbers)

DOI Original · Report dated July 1972; discussed by SRC September 1972; BBC Royal Institution debate June 1973; SSRN posting doi:10.2139/ssrn.6748619 (not opened)

In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised.page unknown (section 3 'Past disappointments')

What changed. Reviewed AI research for the Science Research Council by dividing it into Advanced Automation (A), Computer-based Central Nervous System studies (C) and a bridge category of Building Robots (B), and reported disappointment with past progress, attributing failures in large part to the combinatorial explosion. Beyond Regression: New Tools for Prediction and Analysis in the Behavioral Sciences

Paul J. Werbos · First public 1974-08 (thesis) · Venue: Ph.D. thesis, Harvard University (Committee on Applied Mathematics; degree in statistics) · Pages (i)–(xviii) + sectional pagination I-1 … VI-140, V-1–V-66 (453 PDF pages) · Citations (OpenAlex) 2,784 (checked 2026-11-03)

· Thesis text republished in P. J. Werbos, The Roots of Backpropagation (Wiley, 1994)

"Dynamic feedback" is essentially a technique for calculating derivatives inexpensively, for use with the classic method of steepest descent.p. (xv) (Synopsis)

What changed. Describes new ways of fitting dynamic models to data (multivariate ARMA estimation and a "measurement-noise-only" approach that maximizes long-term predictive power directly) and the "dynamic feedback" algorithm for calculating derivatives inexpensively for steepest descent, including in intricate, nonlinear models. Tests the methods on simulated data and on long-term predictions of national assimilation and mobilization with the Deutsch-Solow model.
