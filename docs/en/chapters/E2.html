Ch. 2 Symbols and perceptrons 1956–74

Symbolic AI and the first neural networks (1956–1974)

Can intelligence be written as search and logic, and how much can a perceptron learn?

Key point

  • The perceptron of 1958 and LISP of 1960 share a chapter. A learning machine written in probability and symbol processing written in recursive functions: the two later traditions each got their original text within two years.
  • Samuel's checkers program (1959) left the sentence that it learned to play better than its author, and Widrow and Hoff's adaptive element (1960) described training by examples. Both are cited by the reinforcement-learning papers of chapter 3.
  • Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons (1969) and Werbos's thesis (1974) could not be obtained for this version, which is why their cards carry no quotation. Whether the chapter ends in a "first winter" is examined with numbers on the winters page.

Bellman's Dynamic Programming (Princeton, 1957) opens the chapter. The book could not be read for this version and its card is empty, but Sutton (1988) in chapter 3 and the Sutton–Barto textbook (1998) in chapter 4 cite it in their reference lists. It is placed as the root of the reinforcement-learning lineage.

Rosenblatt's "The perceptron" appeared in Psychological Review in November 1958. What McCulloch and Pitts had written in symbolic logic, Rosenblatt rewrote in probability theory: for "a hypothetical nervous system, or machine, called a perceptron", a system of randomly connected units can learn to associate specific responses with specific stimuli. Its references list both Hebb 1949 and McCulloch–Pitts 1943; the two traditions that ran separately in chapter 1 meet here.

The next year Samuel's "Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers" (July 1959, IBM Journal) applied two procedures to checkers, rote learning and a generalization procedure that adjusts the coefficients of a scoring polynomial. The sentence is cautious and clear: enough work has been done to verify that a computer can be programmed to learn to play a better game of checkers than the person who wrote the program. Samuel cited Shannon's chess paper and Rosenblatt; Sutton (1988) and Watkins (1989) in chapter 3 cite Samuel. These four papers are the oldest solid lines of the reinforcement-learning lineage.

Two points fall in 1960. In the April Communications of the ACM, McCarthy's "Recursive functions of symbolic expressions" described LISP, a programming system for the IBM 704, through recursive functions of symbolic expressions and conditional expressions; the quotation comes from the author's own re-typeset copy, as the ACM printing could not be opened. Widrow and Hoff's "Adaptive switching circuits", a technical report of 30 June, proposed an adaptive neuron element whose gains are adjusted to minimize average error, trained by "showing it examples of inputs and desired outputs". Sutton 1988 and Barto 1983 in chapter 3 cite the report.

Minsky's "Steps toward Artificial Intelligence" (1961, Proceedings of the IRE) surveyed search, pattern recognition, learning, planning and induction in one article and is cited by Barto 1983. It appeared in the January 1961 issue.

Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons (1969, MIT Press) is the most-cited book in this chapter: Rumelhart 1986, Hopfield 1982, Ackley 1985, Cybenko 1989 and Minsky 1974 all cite it. It is known for showing geometrically which functions a single-layer perceptron cannot represent, but this version could not open the 1969 printing, so the card carries no quotation; the 1988 expanded edition is in an archive but its text and pagination may differ, and it was not used.

Language and backpropagation close the chapter. Winograd's "Understanding natural language" first appeared as MIT AI Technical Report 235 in January 1971 and in Cognitive Psychology in 1972: a system (SHRDLU) that handles English dialogue about a simple robot world with knowledge represented as procedures. Werbos's Harvard thesis "Beyond Regression" (1974) is known for applying chain-rule adjustment of multilayer-network weights to prediction problems in the behavioral sciences; no public copy exists, the card is empty, and the full text is in a 1994 Wiley volume. The priority of backpropagation is treated in a box in chapter 3.

What was and was not checked

Of the nine papers, the journal printings of Rosenblatt, Samuel and Widrow–Hoff were read; McCarthy in the author's re-typeset copy, Winograd in the technical-report version. Bellman, Minsky–Papert and Werbos were not read. Among the period's candidates, Newell, Shaw and Simon's Logic Theorist (1956) and GPS (1959), ELIZA (1966), A* (1968), STRIPS (1971), and the multilayer learning of Ivakhnenko (1965) and Amari (1967) did not pass the two-textbook rule. The absence of several symbolic-AI classics is, we think, largely an effect of the missing Nilsson comparison.

The perceptron: A probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain

Frank F. Rosenblatt · First public 1958-11 (journal issue) · Venue: Psychological Review, 65(6) · Pages 386–408 · Citations (OpenAlex) 12,279 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Received 23 Apr 1958 (not a public date); Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory report VG-1196-G-1, 1958 (ref. 15, called the first full report in footnote 1; date not checked)

The theory has been developed for a hypothetical nervous system, or machine, called a perceptron.p. 387

What changed. Presents a theory, formulated with probability theory rather than symbolic logic, of how information is stored and how it affects recognition, using a hypothetical nervous system called a perceptron. Concludes that a system of randomly connected units can learn to associate specific responses with specific stimuli.

Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers

Arthur L. Samuel · First public 1959-07 (journal issue) · Venue: IBM Journal of Research and Development, 3(3) · Pages 210–229 · Citations (OpenAlex) 4,444 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Reprinted IBM J. Res. Dev. 44(1/2), 2000 (not checked); book reprint pp. 535ff marked 'Originally published in IBM Journal, Vol. 3, No. 3, July, 1959'

Enough work has been done to verify the fact that a computer can be programmed so that it will learn to play a better game of checkers than can be played by the person who wrote the program.p. 211

What changed. Investigates two machine-learning procedures, rote learning and a generalization procedure that adjusts the terms of a scoring polynomial, using checkers. Reports that a program given the rules, a sense of direction, and an incomplete list of parameters learned to play better than its author in 8 to 10 hours of machine-playing time.

Recursive functions of symbolic expressions and their computation by machine, Part I

DOI

A programming system called LISP (for LISt Processor) has been developed for the IBM 704 computer by the Artificial Intelligence group at M.I.T.p. 1 (author's version)

Adaptive switching circuits

Bernard Carlos Widrow, Marcian E. Hoff · First public 1960-06-30 (tech report) · Venue: IRE WESCON Convention Record, 1960, Part 4 · Pages 96–104 · Citations (OpenAlex) 4,009 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · 1960 IRE WESCON Convention Record, Part 4, pp. 96–104 (1960; month not printed on copy)

An approach to such problems is taken in this paper which does not require an explicit use of the truth table.p. 96

What changed. Proposes adaptive switching circuits designed to minimize the average number of errors, using an adaptive neuron element whose gains are set during a training procedure by performance feedback. The designer trains the system by showing it examples of inputs and desired outputs.

Cites (within this list): Samuel 1959, Shannon 1949
Cited by (within this list): Barto et al. 1983, Sutton 1988, Sutton & Barto 1998

Steps toward Artificial Intelligence

Marvin Minsky · First public 1961-01 (journal issue) · Venue: Proceedings of the IRE, 49(1) · Pages 8–30 · Citations (OpenAlex) 1,704 (checked 2026-11-03)

DOI · Received by the IRE 24 Oct 1960 (footnote, not a public date)

Summary—The problems of heuristic programming—of making computers solve really difficult problems—are divided into five main areas: Search, Pattern-Recognition, Learning, Planning, and Induction.p. 8

What changed. Organized the problems of heuristic programming into five areas (Search, Pattern-Recognition, Learning, Planning, and Induction) and reviewed the problem-solving programs and literature of the time under that scheme. The paper argues that pattern recognition, learning, and planning reduce the search a problem-solving machine must otherwise perform.
